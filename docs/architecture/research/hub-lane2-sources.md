# Hub lane 2: sources on modular frontends and sharing data between apps

Primary sources on the modular monolith, micro-frontends, DDD context mapping, Vue and Vite for large apps, Feature-Sliced Design, and three personal-finance apps, mapped to one PWA that grows into a finance hub.

Read-only research on HEAD 7128551, 2026-10-22. Code paths are relative to the HEAD copy. Every web source was downloaded with curl into a scratch folder, turned into plain text and searched there, so each quote below is checked against the loaded page. One source could not be loaded as text and is marked unconfirmed. No real figure or name from the workbook appears here.

The design sits on the Wave 12b target shape ([plan](https://github.com/ipazanin/kuna/blob/cd621ab/docs/features/platform/storage-boundary-plan.md)): repositories per record kind behind one unit of work, `RecordId` identity, the import-boundary spec and the typed pack seam. Where this file says "the pack seam" it means `PackContribution` and `installPack` from that plan, not today's code.

## Recommendation in short

Build the hub as a **modular monolith in one Vite build**: one repo, one PWA, one deploy to GitHub Pages, with each app (company, personal loans, personal spending and budget, later assets) as a module behind a public entry file. Every source that weighs the costs points this way for one developer. Micro-frontends exist to give separate teams separate deploys, and their own author lists the costs this case would pay for nothing.

Between apps, use DDD's context mapping in its plain form:

- A **small shared kernel** of value types every app uses: `Money`, `CivilDate`, `MonthKey`, `Period`, `RecordId`. Keep it small, as Evans says.
- A **published language of facts** that an app offers others, such as "payments from the company to the owner" or "balances that count toward net worth". The company app is the supplier and personal finances the customer.
- Consumers read facts by a **direct call** to the supplier's facts function, not through events. Everything already sits in one browser's memory after a sync, so a query is the cheapest option with the freshest data. Events would need the outbox and inbox machinery Grzybek lists.
- Each consumer has **one translation file** (a small anticorruption layer) that turns the published facts into its own model. That way a change in the company model breaks one file, not the personal app.
- **Separate ways** for anything that has no real link, such as company invoicing and personal budgets.

The **dashboard** is a shell registry: each app contributes dashboard tiles (a pure function that returns its headline facts plus a lazily loaded component) and waiting jobs. The shell renders whatever is registered and imports no app. This is the 12b pack seam one level up, and it should reuse its mechanics (typed key, `install` at start, lazy loaders) but keep its own contribution type. The reason is in "The dashboard" below.

## 2. What the sources say

### Modular monolith

**Grzybek, Primer.** "Monolith is nothing more than a system that has exactly one deployment unit. No less no more." A module "must be independent and interchangeable", "must have everything necessary to provide desired functionality" and "must have defined interface". On the interface: "A Contract is what we make available outside so it is very important. It is an 'entry point' to our module. Good Contract should be unambiguous and contain only what clients of a given contract need." And the module is a business slice, not a technical layer: "the module in the Modular Monolith is a business module that is able to fully provide a set of desired features."

**Grzybek, Integration Styles.** He compares four styles. Shared Database Data: "its biggest disadvantage is a very high coupling. By sharing data, modules share their state which couples them together." And "it is very hard or even impossible to create one, unified data model which will ensure that the requirements of all modules are met." Direct Call: "The module exposes only what is needed. [...] the state of our module is not exposed to the outside", and "In a scenario with one database, it is important to keep the data really in isolation. It means no constrains between tables from separate modules and no transactions between them." Also: "Both modules will use a different language and have different concepts modeled. Therefore, the Anti-Corruption Layer (ACL) should be applied." On temporal coupling: "In the case of a monolith, it is not a problem because this is its nature". Messaging costs: "we're going to need some sort of Event Bus [...] In addition, we will also need job processing mechanisms for internal processing - outbox, inbox, internal commands messages." His heuristic: "choose Direct Call if you must have strong consistency, don't need maximum level of modules autonomy or Data Timeliness is the primary factor."

**Simon Brown.** "if people can't build monoliths properly, microservices won't help" (his 2015 tweet, quoted on the page). On enforcement: "Many teams I've met simply say, 'we enforce this principle through good discipline and code reviews, because we trust our developers'. [...] but we all know what happens when budgets and deadlines start looming ever closer." And a component is "a grouping of related functionality behind a well-defined interface, which resides inside an execution environment".

**Fowler, MonolithFirst.** "Almost all the successful microservice stories have started with a monolith that got too big and was broken up". And: "The logical way is to design a monolith carefully, paying attention to modularity within the software, both at the API boundaries and how the data is stored."

### Micro-frontends and their costs

**Cam Jackson.** Definition: "An architectural style where independently deliverable frontend applications are composed into a greater whole". The headline benefit is organisational: "more scalable organisations with decoupled, autonomous teams". On talking between apps: "we recommend having them communicate as little as possible, as it often reintroduces the sort of inappropriate coupling that we're seeking to avoid in the first place." And: "avoid having any shared state. Just like sharing a database across microservices, as soon as we share our data structures and domain models, we create massive amounts of coupling". On the URL as a channel: "the routes that we choose constitute a contract".

The costs, from "Downsides": payload, "if every micro frontend includes its own copy of React, then we're forcing our customers to download React n times". Sharing dependencies to avoid that brings back "build-time coupling [...] 'we all must use these exact versions of these dependencies'". Environment differences between a standalone and the real container. Operational and governance complexity: "more repositories, more tools, more build/deploy pipelines, more servers, more domains". His closing test: "You should consider whether you have the technical and organisational maturity required to adopt such an approach without creating chaos."

### DDD context mapping (Evans, DDD Reference 2015)

- **Bounded Context (p. 1).** "Explicitly define the context within which a model applies. Explicitly set boundaries in terms of team organization, usage within specific parts of the application, and physical manifestations such as code bases and database schemas."
- **Shared Kernel (p. 31).** "Designate with an explicit boundary some subset of the domain model that the teams agree to share. Keep this kernel small. Within this boundary, include, along with this subset of the model, the subset of code or of the database design associated with that part of the model. This explicitly shared stuff has special status, and shouldn't be changed without consultation with the other team."
- **Customer/Supplier Development (p. 32).** "Establish a clear customer/supplier relationship between the two teams, meaning downstream priorities factor into upstream planning." And: "Jointly developed automated acceptance tests can validate the expected interface from the upstream. Adding these tests to the upstream team's test suite [...] will free the upstream team to make changes without fear of side effects downstream."
- **Conformist (p. 33).** "Eliminate the complexity of translation between bounded contexts by slavishly adhering to the model of the upstream team."
- **Anticorruption Layer (p. 34).** "As a downstream client, create an isolating layer to provide your system with functionality of the upstream system in terms of your own domain model. [...] Internally, the layer translates in one or both directions as necessary between the two models."
- **Open-host Service (p. 35).** "Define a protocol that gives access to your subsystem as a set of services. Open the protocol so that all who need to integrate with you can use it."
- **Published Language (p. 36).** "Direct translation to and from the existing domain models may not be a good solution. [...] If one is used as a data interchange language, it essentially becomes frozen and cannot respond to new development needs. Therefore: Use a well-documented shared language that can express the necessary domain information as a common medium of communication". And: "Published language is often combined with open-host service."
- **Separate Ways (p. 37).** "Integration is always expensive, and sometimes the benefit is small. Therefore: Declare a bounded context to have no connection to the others at all".
- **Domain Events (p. 12).** "Model information about activity in the domain as a series of discrete events. Represent each event as a domain object." The motive he gives is mostly distributed systems: "The state of a distributed system cannot be kept completely consistent at all times."

### Events versus queries (Fowler, 2017)

Event Notification: "it implies a low level of coupling, and is pretty simple to set up. It can become problematic, however, if there really is a logical flow that runs over various event notifications. The problem is that it can be hard to see such a flow as it's not explicit in any program text." Event-Carried State Transfer: "A recipient can then update it's own copy of customer data with the changes [...] An obvious down-side of this pattern is that there's lots of data schlepped around and lots of copies." And "it does involve more complexity on the receiver, since it has to sort out maintaining all the state, when it's usually easier just to call the sender for more information when needed."

### Vue and Vite for a large app

- **Composables.** "a 'composable' is a function that leverages Vue's Composition API to encapsulate and reuse stateful logic." And "Composables can be extracted not only for reuse, but also for code organization."
- **Async components.** "In large applications, we may need to divide the app into smaller chunks and only load a component from the server when it's needed. To make that possible, Vue has a defineAsyncComponent function". And "Bundlers like Vite and webpack also support the syntax (and will use it as bundle split points)".
- **Provide / inject.** "App-level provides are available to all components rendered in the app. This is especially useful when writing plugins". And "If you are working in a large application with many dependency providers [...] it is best to use Symbol injection keys to avoid potential collisions."
- **State management.** A module-level reactive singleton is the documented simple option: "extract the shared state out of the components, and manage it in a global singleton." The warning that matters: "global state that can be arbitrarily mutated by any component is not going to be very maintainable in the long run", so the docs recommend "methods on the store with names that express the intention of the actions". Pinia is for "Stronger conventions for team collaboration", DevTools, HMR and SSR support. The singleton pattern's named risk is SSR only: "the store being a singleton shared across multiple requests."
- **Vue Router.** "In general, it's a good idea to always use dynamic imports for all your routes. When using a bundler like Vite or webpack, this will automatically benefit from code splitting."
- **Vite.** `import.meta.glob`: "Matched files are by default lazy-loaded via dynamic import and will be split into separate chunks during build." Dynamic imports with variables are limited: "Imports must start with ./ or ../" and "variables only represent file names one level deep". Common chunks: "Vite automatically rewrites code-split dynamic import calls with a preload step so that when A is requested, C is fetched in parallel". Chunking is configured "using build.rolldownOptions.output.codeSplitting".

### Feature-Sliced Design

Layers, from most to least responsibility: App, Processes (deprecated), Pages, Widgets, Features, Entities, Shared. The rule: "A module (file) in a slice can only import other slices when they are located on layers strictly below." Slices: "An ideal slice is independent from other slices on its layer (zero coupling) and contains most of the code related to its primary goal (high cohesion)." Public API: "A public API is a contract between a group of modules, like a slice, and the code that uses it. It also acts as a gate, only allowing access to certain objects, and only through that public API." Its goals include "Only the necessary parts of the slice should be exposed", and it warns against `export *`. For entities that must know each other there is a per-consumer API, the `@x` notation: "entity A can declare a separate public API just for entity B". "Try to keep cross-imports to a minimum".

### Personal-finance apps (orientation only)

**Firefly III.** Double entry around accounts: "Accounts are the holders of transactions and each transaction in Firefly III must have a source and a destination account." Account types: asset accounts (default, shared, savings, cash wallet, credit card), expense accounts, revenue accounts and liabilities. "A liability is a debt: either money you owe or money that is owed to you." Liabilities come in "three types, meant to distinguish them: Loan, Debt, Mortgage", and "you can set the start of the debt and the initial amount. You can also set the interest rate and the period over which interest is paid. Firefly III will not automatically calculate the result however". Budgets are separate from categories: "a budget is just another identifier to link withdrawals (only withdrawals) together", and "categories cannot be 'monetized'". Splits: "Any time you create a deposit, transfer or a withdrawal, Firefly III lets you split a transaction into multiple parts." Piggy banks are earmarks: "Each piggy bank is tied to an asset account on which its money is saved." Separate books are on the way: "Firefly III is working towards support for multiple financial administrations. This means that you will be able to keep track of multiple separated financial sets of data", each with "the primary currency for your administration". The page says "This is not yet finished work".

**Actual Budget.** Accounts split by whether they feed the budget: "Off budget accounts don't affect the budget and are meant to track stuff like investments and mortgages. Transactions in off budget accounts can't be categorized; they simply track balances over time." On-budget accounts "affect the budget, and transactions can be categorized". Envelope budgeting: "You can think of categories as little funds that you deposit money into." and "Your total account balance is available to budget." Schedules plan recurring payments: "suppose you have a mortgage payment that is automatically deducted from your bank account each month. In that case, you can create a schedule for that payment and link it to the corresponding transaction".

**hledger.** Five account types: "Asset A things you own, Liability L things you owe, Equity E owner's investment, balances the two above" and "Revenue R inflows", "Expense X outflows", plus subtypes "Cash C liquid assets", "Conversion V" and "Gain G capital gains/losses". Holdings are amounts in another commodity: "Amounts in hledger have both a 'quantity', which is a signed decimal number, and a 'commodity', which is a currency symbol, stock ticker, or any word or phrase describing something you are tracking." Prices are dated directives such as `P 2024-03-01 AAPL $179`.

**What the three share.** An account with a type (asset or liability at least), transactions between accounts, transfers between your own accounts as their own kind, categories for spending, budgets per period on top of categories, liabilities as accounts with a principal, start and interest rate, and holdings as quantities of a commodity with dated prices. Firefly and hledger put every amount in a currency or commodity.

## 4. Sharing data between apps without coupling

**The kernel.** Value types only, no records: `Money` (with a currency once assets arrive), `CivilDate`, `MonthKey`, `Period`, `RecordId`, and one small `BalanceKind` of asset or liability, taken from the vocabulary all three finance apps share. Accounts, categories and counterparties stay inside each app. A company category such as "owner pay" means something different from a personal category such as "salary", which is Grzybek's warning that "one, unified data model" ends "very weak, ambiguous".

**The published language.** Each app exports one facts entry point, typed, read-only and small, as Grzybek's contract that should "contain only what clients of a given contract need". For the company app, a first cut:

```ts
// apps/company/facts.ts: the only file another app may import from the company app
export interface OwnerPayment {
  readonly id: RecordId
  readonly paidOn: CivilDate
  readonly kind: 'salary' | 'dividend' | 'loan-to-owner' | 'loan-repaid-by-owner' | 'reimbursement' | 'loan-from-owner'
  readonly amount: Money
}
export interface NetWorthLine {
  readonly label: string
  readonly kind: BalanceKind
  readonly amount: Money
  readonly asOf: CivilDate
}
export function ownerPayments(period: Period): readonly OwnerPayment[]
export function netWorthLines(asOf: CivilDate): readonly NetWorthLine[]
```

This is a sketch for orientation, not a design. The names are placeholders until the per-app discussions.

**Queries, not events.** A consumer calls the supplier's facts function when it computes. Nothing is copied into its storage. Fowler's reason fits: copying means "lots of copies" and the receiver "has to sort out maintaining all the state, when it's usually easier just to call the sender". The cost: personal screens that read company facts need the company workbook loaded, which is question 2.

**One translation file per consumer.** For example `apps/personal/infrastructure/companyFacts.ts` turns `OwnerPayment` into whatever personal finances calls income or a loan movement. Only that file imports `apps/company/facts.ts`, and the boundary spec says so.

**The same money seen twice.** A net salary leaves the company account and lands on the owner's personal account. If personal finances imports its own bank statements, the payment is already there as a personal transaction. The company fact then labels or matches it (like invoice matching today), rather than adding a second entry. Firefly treats a move between your own accounts as a transfer, not as income and an expense. The dashboard must also not add the company's cash and the owner's claim on the company as if both were the owner's. This decides what the facts are for, and it is question 3.

**Who owns a record seen from both sides.** A loan between the owner and the company is a liability for one and a receivable for the other. The company app already derives the company's side from ledger rows (`ownerLoans.ts:74-83`, `ownerAdvances.ts:60-61`). The plain rule from Grzybek and Evans is one owner per record and a published fact for the other side, never two stored copies. Which side owns it is question 4.

## 5. The dashboard

What the sources give:

- Jackson's composition is a container that mounts apps and passes "callbacks and data downwards", which "makes the contract more explicit" than custom events. Inside one build the same idea is a registry the shell reads.
- Vue's `defineAsyncComponent` and Vite's split points let each tile load only when the dashboard shows it, and Vite preloads shared chunks in parallel.
- FSD's App layer "combines all business domains", which is the shell's place: it may know every app's entry file, and no app may know the shell's internals.

A hub contribution would look like the 12b pack contribution (`plan.md`, "The pack seam"): a typed object an app builds, installed by `main.ts` with `await import`, holding screens, waiting jobs, cards and settings tabs as lazy loaders. The dashboard adds two slots: tiles, each a lazy component plus a pure headline function, and the net worth lines.

**Why not one contribution type for both levels.** The pack extends the company model: `assemble` takes the core `Workbook` and `waitingJobs(core: Workbook, part: Part, ...)` reads it (plan.md, "The pack seam"). That is a shared model, and it is correct inside one bounded context. An app must not get another app's model, only its facts. If the hub reused `PackContribution`, every app would be handed the company `Workbook`, which is the coupling the context map is there to stop. So reuse the mechanics (typed key, install function, lazy loaders, the boundary spec) and keep two types: the pack seam inside the company app, the app seam at the shell.

**Discovery.** `import.meta.glob('./apps/*/app.ts')` could find apps by folder. An explicit list of four imports in `main.ts` is plainer, searchable and shows the order, so I would use the list.

**URL as a contract.** Jackson: "the routes that we choose constitute a contract". With prefixes per app (`#/company/...`, `#/personal/...`) a dashboard tile links into an app by route, with no import. The pack's `costGroupLinks` in 12b already returns a `RouteLocationRaw` for the same reason.

## 6. Questions for the owner

1. **One Google Sheet for everything, or one per app?** Today the company workbook is a Sheet the morning ingest writes to. Personal loans and budgets could be new tabs in it, or a second Sheet, or records kept only in this browser. A second Sheet keeps the company's books apart from your private ones and doubles the Picker and sync work. Same Sheet is one sync and one file to back up, but your personal records would sit in the company's file. I lean to one Sheet per app, behind the same storage port. Which do you want?
2. **May personal screens need the company workbook loaded?** With queries, personal figures that use owner payments are only right after a company sync. The other way copies the facts into personal storage on each sync, which is more code and two copies. I lean to queries, since you load everything on the same phone anyway.
3. **Are company facts a second source of entries, or labels on your personal bank rows?** If you will import personal bank statements, salary and dividends already arrive there. The company facts would then match and label those rows, not add new ones. If you will not import personal statements, the facts become the entries. Which is it?
4. **Who owns a loan between you and the company?** The company app already derives both directions from its ledger rows. I would keep the company app as the owner and have personal loans read it as a fact. The alternative is entering it in personal loans and having the company read it, which turns the dependency around. This changes record ownership, so I am asking rather than assuming.
5. **Should `Money` carry a currency now?** Budgets and loans in euros work without it. Stocks and foreign accounts need it. Adding it to the kernel later touches every app, adding it now touches about every file that does money maths. I would add it in the refactoring wave, before the second app exists.
6. **What is the dashboard's headline?** Personal net worth, with your share of the company as one line, or a consolidated view that adds company cash and your personal accounts? The first avoids counting the same money twice. The second answers "how much do I and my company have".
